Yes—but there is an interesting wrinkle: **there doesn't seem to be a well-established "Precision Teaching keyboarding curriculum" in the way there are PT materials for reading, math facts, spelling, etc.** However, typing is actually an unusually good candidate for Precision Teaching because it is a highly measurable motor/academic skill with a natural rate measure.

I found several pieces that point pretty strongly in that direction.

### **The basic PT approach maps beautifully onto typing**

The modern description of Precision Teaching boils down to:

1. **Pinpoint** a very specific behavior.  
2. **Arrange practice** for that behavior.  
3. **Time and measure** performance.  
4. **Chart** the frequency.  
5. **Make instructional decisions** from the data and try something different if progress stalls.

That is almost tailor-made for keyboarding.

For example, instead of:

> "Practice typing."

you might have pinpoints such as:

* `Types F when shown F`  
* `Types J when shown J`  
* `Types F-J-F-J...`  
* `Types words using ASDF`  
* `Types common digraphs`  
* `Types words from the current vocabulary`  
* `Types unfamiliar words without looking at the keyboard`  
* `Types dictated words`  
* `Types dictated sentences`

And then you measure **correct responses per minute and incorrect responses per minute**.

That last distinction is particularly important. PT isn't simply "get faster at typing." You're looking for a **high and increasing correct rate while keeping errors low**.

### **And there actually is a historical connection to typing**

This was particularly interesting.

A historical discussion of Precision Teaching points out that **August Dvorak's scientific approach to typing instruction predates Precision Teaching and contained many of the same features**: timed probes, warm-ups, emphasis on response frequency, modeling, reinforcement, individual data collection, charting, and self-recording.

And a Precision Teaching paper explicitly uses typing as an example of a skill where rate measurement makes sense.

There's also a very concrete example from the **Ben Bronz Academy**, whose program grew directly out of the Precision Teaching tradition: students practiced **touch typing in one-minute fluencies**, recorded their results, and tracked their improvement on charts.

So you're not trying to force an alien methodology onto typing. **Typing has actually been part of the fluency tradition for quite a long time.**

---

## **What I think a PT typing program should look like**

I would *not* start by having students type paragraphs for one minute and simply record WPM.

That's useful as an outcome measure, but it isn't particularly good Precision Teaching.

Instead, I'd build a **component → composite** progression.

### **Stage 1: Establish the keyboard responses**

For example:

| Pinpoint | Measurement |
| ----- | ----- |
| Types `f` when shown `f` | correct/min |
| Types `j` when shown `j` | correct/min |
| Types `f` or `j` when shown either | correct/min |
| Types `a s d f` when shown | correct/min |
| Types `j k l ;` when shown | correct/min |
| Types home-row sequences | correct/min |

You could use very short timings initially—perhaps **10 or 15 seconds**—rather than immediately asking a beginner to struggle through a full minute.

Short "sprints" are consistent with fluency-building procedures described in the PT literature.

The key is that the student is producing **lots of responses**, getting immediate feedback, and trying again.

---

## **Stage 2: Build the individual letter movements**

This is where I think PT could be especially powerful.

Suppose a student knows the home row but keeps hesitating on `g` and `h`.

Rather than making the student do another 10-minute typing lesson, you could isolate the component:

> `f g f g f g f g...`

or

> `h j h j h j...`

Then:

> `f g h j f g h j...`

You're essentially building **motor fluency**.

The research literature describes Precision Teaching as being applicable to component motor movements that eventually combine into more complex behaviors; one example explicitly gives **typing** as a composite behavior built from component movements.

That's a very PT way of thinking about keyboarding.

---

## **Stage 3: Move from letters to chunks**

This is where I think a conventional typing curriculum and PT would diverge somewhat.

Instead of primarily practicing:

> `aaaa ssss dddd ffff`

I'd progressively introduce **useful letter combinations**:

th th th th  
he he he he  
in in in in  
er er er er  
an an an an  
re re re re

Then:

the the the the  
and and and and  
that that that

Then actual words.

This begins to build **generative fluency**, rather than merely making the student's fingers fast at artificial exercises.

The distinction matters because the ultimate behavior isn't "press keys quickly." It's something closer to:

> **See/think language → produce written language fluently through the keyboard.**

Precision Teaching's emphasis on component skills and then combining them into increasingly complex repertoires fits this very nicely.

---

# **One thing I would measure separately: accuracy**

I'd strongly recommend recording two numbers:

**Correct responses/minute**

and

**Incorrect responses/minute**

For example:

> 42 correct / 3 incorrect

rather than simply:

> 93% accuracy

The PT perspective is interested in **frequency/rate**, and Standard Celeration Charts make changes in rate over time much easier to see than conventional percentage-correct scores.

For typing, you could eventually have a student's chart showing something like:

Correct typing  
10 → 14 → 18 → 23 → 27 → 32 → 38 → 44/min

Errors  
7 → 5 → 4 → 3 → 2 → 2 → 1 → 1/min

That tells you much more than:

> "Johnny is 87% accurate."

---

# **And then there's the really interesting question: the fluency aim**

This is where I'd be cautious about simply importing a conventional **40 WPM \= fluent** rule.

There are published Precision Teaching frequency aims for some academic behaviors, but **I didn't find a well-established, PT-specific frequency aim for touch typing**. PT literature does contain examples of typing frequency ranges, including a cited example of **60–90 words per minute** in the context of behavioral fluency.

But I would treat that as a **starting hypothesis**, not a scientifically established universal typing aim.

The better PT question is:

> **At what rate does this particular typing behavior become durable, effortless, and useful?**

Because behavioral fluency isn't merely speed. The broader concept includes things such as **retention, endurance, and application/generalization**.

That's particularly important for typing.

A student who can type 70 WPM for 30 seconds but falls apart after five minutes isn't necessarily fluent.

---

# **I would therefore have several different "fluencies"**

For a school program, I'd probably distinguish:

### **1\. Acquisition fluency**

Can the student produce the keystrokes correctly and rapidly?

### **2\. Word fluency**

Can the student type ordinary words rapidly without conscious searching?

### **3\. Composition fluency**

Can the student generate their own language while typing?

### **4\. Endurance**

Can they maintain the performance for several minutes?

### **5\. Transfer**

Can they maintain the skill when typing something they haven't practiced?

That last one is crucial.

A student who gets 80 WPM on a familiar typing test isn't necessarily an 80-WPM typist.

---

## **The daily routine could actually be very short**

I can imagine something like a **10-minute daily PT typing period**:

**1 minute — baseline timing**

Type a standardized probe.

**2 minutes — component practice**

Work on whatever the chart says is weak.

**2 minutes — targeted fluency practice**

For example, `th`, `he`, `ing`, etc.

**1 minute — break / reset**

**2 minutes — word or sentence practice**

**1 minute — final timing**

**1 minute — record/chart**

The critical PT feature is that **the data determine what happens tomorrow**.

If the student is accelerating nicely, don't mess with it.

If performance flatlines:

> Change something.

Perhaps the exercise is too difficult. Perhaps the stimulus arrangement is wrong. Perhaps the student needs a smaller component. Perhaps they're looking at the keyboard. Perhaps they're making the same particular error repeatedly.

That "try, try again" aspect is fundamental to PT.

## **One particularly promising possibility for you**

Given that you're interested in Precision Teaching, I think **typing could make an excellent little PT experiment rather than simply adopting an existing typing curriculum**.

You could take something like a conventional touch-typing sequence for the *instructional content*, but build a PT system around it:

**Conventional curriculum tells you what to introduce.**

**Precision Teaching tells you how to measure, practice, and decide what happens next.**

That's actually quite faithful to the modern conception of PT: **Precision Teaching isn't itself a curriculum or a prescribed instructional program.** It's a measurement and decision-making system that can be applied to different curricula.

And there is already a substantial body of research supporting PT as a general fluency-building and instructional decision-making system, although the literature specifically studying *PT-based touch-typing curricula* appears to be much thinner.

# **You could have several kinds of tests**

### **1\. Whole-text typing**

Give everyone the same passage:

> The quick brown fox...

This is your conventional typing assessment.

But you could make it much more PT-like by having **different passages for different purposes**.

For example:

**Acquisition probe**

asdf jkl; asdf jkl; asdf jkl;

**Word probe**

the and that with this from they

**Generalization probe**

A passage the student has never seen.

**Composition probe**

Instead of displaying text, give:

> Write three sentences explaining your favorite season.

That measures something quite different.

---

# **2\. You could test *specific components***

This is where I think your idea gets really interesting.

You could tell the program:

> Test: `g` and `h`

and have it generate:

ghghghghghghghghghghghgh

Or:

> Test: `th`

and get:

th th th th th th th th

Or:

> Test: words containing `th`

and have it select from:

the  
that  
this  
there  
think  
three  
through  
another

Now you're doing something much closer to **Precision Teaching component analysis**.

The literature explicitly describes PT timings as counting both correct and incorrect responses and converting them to a per-minute frequency; one-minute timings are common, although 15- and 30-second timings are also used.

---

# **3\. I would make the timing completely configurable**

For example, the teacher could select:

| Timing | Use |
| ----- | ----- |
| 10 sec | Very early acquisition |
| 15 sec | Component practice |
| 30 sec | Short fluency timing |
| 60 sec | Standard assessment |
| 2 min | Endurance |
| 5 min | Endurance/generalization |

And the program would automatically convert everything to **responses per minute**.

So a student who types:

> 37 correct in 30 seconds

gets recorded as:

> **74 correct/min**

That's much more useful for your PT charts than having different raw numbers for different timing periods.

---

# **The really important part: count the keystrokes**

I wouldn't use ordinary WPM as the primary measurement.

I'd record:

**Correct responses/minute**

**Incorrect responses/minute**

and perhaps calculate WPM as a secondary measure.

That's much closer to Precision Teaching. Conventional typing sites tend to obscure what's actually happening by reducing everything to a single WPM/accuracy score.

For example:

Student: Sarah

Timing: 60 sec  
Correct: 63  
Incorrect: 4

Frequency:  
63 correct/min  
4 incorrect/min

That's excellent PT data.

And you could put those numbers directly onto a Standard Celeration Chart.

The reason this is valuable is that percentage accuracy can hide enormous differences in fluency. PT's use of response frequency is specifically intended to expose those differences.

---

# **There's another measurement I would add: errors by key**

This would be **extremely useful instructionally**.

Suppose Sarah's 60-second timing produces:

Correct: 72  
Incorrect: 5

But the program knows the errors were:

g   3  
b   1  
p   1

You could show:

ERRORS

g    ███  
b    █  
p    █

Or simply:

> Most frequent error: **g**

Then tomorrow's practice can target `g`.

That's exactly the kind of **data → instructional decision** loop that makes PT interesting.

# **I'd actually build a "teacher mode"**

This is the part that makes me think you don't need to compromise with existing typing tutors.

You could have a teacher dashboard where you define a test:

### **Test definition**

**Name**

> Home Row 1

**Stimulus**

asdf jkl;

**Response type**

> Type exact text repeatedly

**Timing**

> 30 seconds

**Scoring**

> Correct/incorrect keystrokes

**Aim**

> 100–120 correct/min

**Errors allowed**

> ≤ 2/min

Then:

**Save Test**

And now you've got a reusable assessment.

---

# **And you could create "slices"**

This is particularly compatible with the Morningside/Precision Teaching way of thinking.

Suppose your students are learning:

asdf  
jkl;  
qwer  
uiop  
zxcv  
nm,.

You could have:

### **Slice A — current material**

asdf jkl;

### **Slice B — cumulative**

asdf jkl; qwer uiop

### **Slice C — review**

Randomly sample everything learned so far.

Then your students aren't merely getting better at the exact exercise they've practiced.

You're periodically asking:

> **Can you actually perform the skill across the repertoire?**

That's much closer to a genuine fluency assessment.

---

# **I would also separate "practice" from "assessment"**

This is important.

The same application could have two modes.

### **Practice mode**

Student gets immediate feedback.

asdf jkl; asdf jkl;  
    ^  
    ERROR: expected "f"

They can stop and try again.

### **Assessment mode**

No feedback.

Student types.

Timer runs.

At the end:

74 correct  
3 incorrect

That's it.

That distinction is very important if you're trying to determine whether the behavior is actually fluent rather than merely whether the student can perform with continuous feedback.

Some PT studies explicitly conduct timings without instruction or teaching during the timing itself and record correct and incorrect rates.

## **One thing I'd deliberately *not* do**

I wouldn't make students log into a complicated system and navigate through lessons.

I'd want the student experience to be almost ridiculously simple:

> **Open link → identify yourself → click START → type → results.**

Everything interesting happens on the teacher/data side.

That would also let you use the exact same system for things besides typing.

For example, you could eventually use the same timing engine for:

* typing  
* math facts  
* letter naming  
* vocabulary  
* spelling  
* multiplication  
* coding syntax  
* reading word lists

The underlying application doesn't really care what the behavior is.

It just needs to know:

> **What stimulus is presented, what constitutes a correct response, how long to time it, and how to count the responses.**

That's essentially a little **Precision Teaching measurement engine**.

### **I think this is worth building.**

Given that you have Chromebooks and Google Workspace, I'd probably make the first version **an Apps Script web app backed by a Google Sheet**, rather than trying to find an existing service. It would be small enough that we could make the teacher interface quite sophisticated while keeping the student interface almost completely bare.

And importantly, **you could change the tests yourself without modifying the program**—for example, by editing rows in a Google Sheet.
